“How might we create a low-pressure, student-friendly space that helps college students recognise what they are feeling, reflect on their wellbeing and choose an appropriate pathway to support?”

Why Student Wellbeing & How We Approached It

Grounded in the SRMIST Design Thinking & Methodology curriculum (21DCS201P), SafeSense moves from human-centred problem framing to a working software prototype without overclaiming clinical or institutional status.

01 · Context & Problem Statement

The Everyday Campus Silence

University students frequently navigate overlapping pressures—semester exams, placement preparation, sleep disruption, homesickness, financial worries, and social isolation. While formal campus counselling exists, many students hesitate to reach out early because they feel judged, struggle to articulate what they are experiencing, or assume their distress is “not serious enough” for an appointment.

Core Design Gap: Between silent self-struggle and formal clinical counselling, students lack a gentle, stigma-free middle step to notice patterns and express feelings safely.
02 · Governance & Privacy Boundaries

Three-Tier Consent Architecture

  • Student Autonomy First: Students retain control over what they log, reflect on, and choose to disclose.
  • Deliberate Counsellor Handoff: Counsellors receive a support request only when a student explicitly fills out a request and consents.
  • Zero-Surveillance Administration: College administration would only view privacy-protected, aggregated wellbeing trends—never private check-in text or identifiable conversations.
  • Honest Current Status: None of these institutional integrations are live or adopted by SRMIST yet; they represent our target system design.

Core Design Principles Guiding SafeSense

Derived from Dieter Rams’ Good Design & Human-Centred Methodology
P1

Low-Friction Emotional Entry

Replace intimidating clinical intake forms with a five-point mood check-in and familiar campus tags (Exams, Sleep, Placements, Homesick).

P2

Guided Articulation Without Diagnosis

Offer gentle conversational prompts (“Sense”) to help students name feelings when words fail, while explicitly stating it is not a therapist.

P3

Consent-Gated Escalation

Keep personal reflections private by default; human counselling outreach happens only through an intentional, student-initiated step.

P4

Epistemic & Academic Honesty

Clearly separate built prototype functionality from conceptual features, unvalidated hypotheses, and proposed future institutional workflows.

ACTIVITY 01

Problem Identification

Completed · Problem Framing

1. Professor’s Task / Question

Identify a specific user group experiencing everyday health or wellbeing challenges, uncover three daily difficulties they face, select the single most critical problem, brainstorm three solution directions, and propose one named concept with a one-line tagline—without jumping prematurely to hardware.

2. What Our Team Explored & Did

Using the SCOPES framing rubric (Situation, Constraints, Objectives, People, Estimates, Scope), our 9-member team focused on college students navigating academic stress, anxiety, loneliness, and hesitation to ask for help. We compared three candidate directions before defining SafeSense.

3. Reasoning Behind Our Approach

Rather than building an invasive biometric wearable or clinical diagnostic tool, we recognised that the primary bottleneck for students is emotional friction: not knowing how to name feelings or take a safe first step toward support.

5. Outcome & Learning

Defined our initial concept SafeSense with the tagline: “A gentle, student-friendly space to recognise feelings, reflect safely, and choose support.” Learned that framing the right “How Might We” question prevents over-engineered solutions.

6. How It Influenced the Next Stage of SafeSense

Established the non-diagnostic, student-controlled scope that anchored our mind map (Activity 04), persona (Activity 05), and eventual browser-based prototype architecture (Activity 10).

ACTIVITY 02

Visualisation

Completed · Individual Future-Vision Exercise
Methodological Clarification: Activity 02 was an individual future-vision exercise (“Where do you see yourself 10 years from now?”), not a SafeSense product-feature planning session. In keeping with strict academic honesty, individual team members’ personal drawings are not fabricated here.

1. Professor’s Task / Question

“Where do you see yourself 10 years from now?” Each student individually created a visual chart representing personal and professional aspirations using imagery, symbols, and spatial connections rather than paragraphs of text, then shared within the team.

2. What Our Team Explored & Did

All nine members independently sketched 10-year life and career maps on chart paper, presented them inside our group without judgement, and selected one representative chart to present to the class based on clarity and visual storytelling.

3. Reasoning Behind Our Approach

Design thinking requires translating abstract internal states into concrete visual artefacts. Practising non-verbal visual expression on personal goals built empathy for how hard it is to articulate complex emotions in plain sentences.

5. Outcome & Learning

Team members experienced firsthand how visual metaphors communicate nuance faster than text, and how non-judgemental peer sharing builds psychological safety within a newly formed 9-person group.

6. How It Influenced the Next Stage of SafeSense

Directly shaped SafeSense’s UI philosophy: instead of forcing students to write long essays about distress, the prototype uses visual emoji scales, quick concern tags, and weekly mood bar charts to make emotional patterns visible at a glance.

ACTIVITY 03

Creativity

Completed · Creative Fluency & Peer Critique

1. Professor’s Task / Question

Overcome creative blocks through timed constraint exercises (such as the 30 Circles Challenge) and structured “Pitch, Question & Build” peer feedback rounds while practising core brainstorming norms.

2. What Our Team Explored & Did

We practised rapid visual association under time constraints and conducted structured 3–4 minute problem pitches followed by “Have you considered…?” and “What if the user…?” questioning rounds.

3. Reasoning Behind Our Approach

Early self-censorship kills unconventional ideas, especially on sensitive topics like mental health. Separating idea generation from critique allowed our 9-member team to explore gentle, non-clinical ways to support students.

5. Outcome & Learning

Shifted team mindset from “defending our first idea” to “questioning assumptions.” Peer questions highlighted the need for anonymity and low-effort interaction when a student feels overwhelmed.

6. How It Influenced the Next Stage of SafeSense

Inspired the multi-pathway structure of SafeSense—recognising that different students need different entry doors (private logging vs. guided prompts vs. peer rooms vs. human counselling).

Stakeholder Analysis

Completed · Ecosystem & Mendelow Matrix
Institutional Status Note: This stakeholder analysis models the campus ecosystem around SafeSense. Neither SRMIST administration nor the college counselling committee has officially adopted, approved, or integrated SafeSense.

1. Professor’s Task / Question

“Who Matters to Your Product?” Identify primary and secondary stakeholders across the ecosystem, classify their power and interest using Mendelow’s 2×2 Matrix, and define tailored engagement strategies.

2. What Our Team Explored & Did

We analysed five core stakeholder groups: Students, College Counselling Committee, College Administration, Faculty/Academic Advisors, and Peer/Student Support Networks, mapping their relationships and governance boundaries.

Journey Mapping (Dual Stakeholder Perspectives)

Completed · Two Distinct Journey Maps

1. Professor’s Task / Question

“Walk in Their Shoes.” Select two stakeholders—(1) the primary user and (2) a high-power/high-interest decision-maker—and create two separate journey maps tracking actions, thoughts, feelings, pain points, and three concrete improvements for each.

2. What Our Team Explored & Did

We mapped two completely separate journeys: Journey A follows an overwhelmed student exploring SafeSense late at night; Journey B follows a College Counselling / Student Affairs decision-maker evaluating SafeSense for campus adoption.

3. Reasoning Behind Our Approach

Combining user and institutional journeys hides critical friction. Students worry about judgement and emotional effort, while decision-makers worry about privacy liability, safeguarding, and counsellor workload.

5. Outcome & Learning

Uncovered six distinct design improvements—three lowering emotional friction for students and three strengthening consent transparency, crisis boundaries, and workflow clarity for campus leaders.

6. How It Influenced the Next Stage of SafeSense

Motivated the persistent “Need help now?” button, explicit privacy reassurance (“Your check-ins stay private unless you choose to share them”), and structured conversation starters in our prototype.

PESTEL Analysis — Future Boosters & Setbacks
Booster · Social & Academic

Rising academic competition, placement pressure, and post-transition isolation increase demand for accessible student wellbeing tools.

Booster · Technological

Ubiquitous smartphone browser access allows zero-install, low-friction daily check-ins on campus.

Booster · Political / Institutional

Growing higher-education policy emphasis on student mental health and campus counselling infrastructure.

Setback · Legal & Privacy

Strict data-protection expectations (e.g., DPDP Act principles) require rigorous consent and encryption before handling sensitive student reflections.

Setback · Economical & Operational

Limited campus counselling staff capacity and peer-moderation costs can constrain institutional rollout if workflows are poorly scoped.

Setback · Environmental / Digital Fatigue

Screen fatigue and notification overload may reduce long-term engagement if check-ins feel burdensome.

01 · Home
Daily Check-In & Navigation

Greets the student with “How are you feeling right now?”, offers 5 mood options (Great, Good, Okay, Low, Rough), 8 concern tags (Exams, Sleep, Friends, Family, Placements, Health, Homesick, Money), and an optional reflection box.

Supportive Conversation Guide

05 · Counsellor
Opt-In Human Support Pathway

Allows students to enter their college name, select support type (e.g., Student Counselling Cell), and submit a connection request with the explicit assurance that check-ins stay private unless shared.

User Storyboard — From Quiet Stress to Supported Choice
  1. Scene 1 (Trigger): Late at night during exam week, a student feels anxious and unable to sleep, unsure how to explain what is wrong.
  2. Scene 2 (Check-In): Opens SafeSense on mobile browser, taps “Low” + “Exams” + “Sleep” in under 20 seconds.
  3. Scene 3 (Reflect & Express): Checks the Mood tab, notices a midweek dip, and taps “Talk to Sense” using starter prompts.
  4. Scene 4 (Decide): Explores Together topic rooms to feel less isolated, or opens the Counsellor tab to deliberately request human support.

Honest Prototype Limitations (Mandatory Academic Disclosure)
  • Built-in Guide Only: Sense is a built-in supportive conversation guide—it is not connected to an external generative AI service.
  • Local Form Storage Only: Counselling requests are stored locally in the current browser prototype and are not transmitted to SRMIST counsellors.
  • Single-User Community Preview: Together posts and rooms are not currently shared live with other students.
  • Not Clinically Validated: SafeSense has not undergone clinical trials or formal psychological efficacy validation.
  • Not a Therapist or Crisis Service: It is not a diagnostic tool, medical device, or emergency/crisis intervention service.
  • No Live Institutional Integration: There is no confirmed live integration with SRMIST administration or counselling systems.

Final Reflection & Academic Conclusion

What Design Thinking Taught Us About Designing for Student Wellbeing

Across all 13 activities, SafeSense evolved from a broad concern about student stress into a disciplined, human-centred software concept. The most important lesson of our semester was restraint: recognising that when designing around emotional wellbeing, trust, clarity, and honest boundaries matter more than technical complexity.

By separating private student reflection from consent-gated counselling requests and anonymised institutional trends, SafeSense respects both student autonomy and campus safeguarding realities. Our live browser prototype demonstrates the core check-in, reflection, and support-routing experience, while transparently documenting what remains to be validated—through structured SRMIST student usability testing, counselling committee workflow review, and ethical privacy governance—before any real-world deployment.
